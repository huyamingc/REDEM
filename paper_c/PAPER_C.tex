\author{Yaming Hu\fnref{orcid}}

\begin{abstract}
Online learning systems couple several adaptive mechanisms, but which
is responsible for which capability --- and whether any can
be transplanted to another substrate --- is rarely tested. We dissect
three mechanisms of a reservoir-based online learner: a slow-trace
statistical memory (M3), a homeostatic chaos regulator (M5), and the raw
physics of the substrate. A falsifying transfer experiment (an
echo-state network with and without the metadata under a sequential
disturbance chain, 10 seeds) shows that the $+32\%$ sequential-recovery
gain of the substrate system is the homeostat's, not the metadata's. The
metadata does not transfer memory-capacity robustness to an
ESN at any tested timescale ($\tau_m \in \{200,500,1000,2000\}$;
paired differences $-0.68$ to $-0.70$ across $\tau_m$, 0/10 seeds positive). Its
transferable value is narrower and precisely located: it denoises
\emph{state}-level corruption (gap $-0.69 \rightarrow -0.04$ to $-0.01$
when noise enters before the slow trace, at every tested $\tau_m$) and
accelerates boundary adaptation by a controlled-measured 1.25--2.7$\times$
on the substrate and $\sim$10 pulses on the ESN. Raw
memory capacity remains a substrate property (MC 10.19 vs.\ 6.17). Three
mechanisms, three roles --- none transferable to another's job in the
directions and conditions tested.
A controlled proof of concept on a Transformer with low-rank
adapters confirms the boundary on a second substrate: slow-trace domain
routing transfers (forgetting up to $-28\%$, stream perplexity $-1.07$ at
$\tau_m{=}200$; 10/10 seeds; the stream-perplexity benefit holds only at
$\tau_m\le500$ and reverses sign at $\tau_m\ge1000$) while a gating-only variant is falsified at
every tested $\tau_m$ (0/10 seeds) --- detection without continuous
correction is insufficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Frozen batch models --- including large foundation models --- cannot adapt
to post-deployment reality without expensive retraining
\cite{parisi2019}; online reservoirs can
\cite{jaeger2001,maass2002,lukosevicius2009},
but their readouts face a timescale gap on long-horizon statistical tasks:
fast reservoir states decorrelate far more quickly than the task
statistics, so the readout must re-integrate after every change. Our
companion work introduces a continuously active recursive-least-squares
(RLS) readout (M1), a slow
exponential trace of the reservoir states (metadata, M3), a homeostatic
regulator that keeps the substrate near the edge of chaos (M5), and slow
structure plasticity (M4); the integrated
system tracks drift where frozen learners fail permanently
(companion Paper~B) \cite{companionB}.

These mechanisms are usually validated \emph{together}. The question we
ask here is sharper: \emph{which mechanism does which job, and can any of
them be transplanted to another substrate?} We answer with a deliberately
falsifying experiment: take a generic echo-state network (ESN), give it
the same metadata, run it through the same sequential disturbance chain,
and measure what transfers. The answer is largely negative and,
we argue, informative: the metadata is a substrate-agnostic
\emph{statistical} memory that transfers \emph{accuracy} and
\emph{state-noise denoising}, but not \emph{memory-capacity robustness}
under readout-level disturbance; sequential recovery is the homeostat's
job; raw memory capacity is the substrate's physics. Three mechanisms,
three roles --- none substitutable in the directions tested. Section~\ref{sec:llm} tests whether
this decomposition survives on a structurally different substrate --- a
Transformer with low-rank adapters --- and reports one transferable
instantiation (routing) and one falsified one (gating-only).

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Reservoir family.} We compare (i)~an ESN-256
\cite{lukosevicius2012} with heterogeneous
leak rates matched to a log-normal spectrum (coefficient of variation
$\mathrm{CV}=0.20$, spectral
radius 0.9, within the echo-state regime \cite{yildiz2012}) and (ii)~a Si$_3$N$_4$-style relaxation substrate whose
per-unit time constants are log-normal (median $\tau_0\approx174\,\mu$s,
$\mathrm{CV}=0.20$), driven in per-pulse contrast-coupled dynamics with
coupling $\kappa$ (initialized at $\kappa_0=25.3$; the homeostat
regulates $\kappa$ on a finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) criterion,
with the regulation law and threshold specified in companion Paper~A)
\cite{companionA}. Both produce a
fast state
$x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{256}$ per pulse. All algorithmic timescales
($\tau_m$, $\tau_f$, the regime length $L$) are expressed in pulses:
both substrates are driven and compared on this common per-pulse clock,
and the physical-time interpretation of the substrate pulse (median
per-unit $\tau_0\approx174\,\mu$s) is given in companion Paper~A. A
spectral-radius sensitivity scan (0.7/0.9/0.99; 120 runs) exists in the
released scripts but is not tabulated in this paper; the reported
results are conditional on the chosen configuration (radius 0.9,
$\mathrm{CV}=0.20$).

\textbf{Slow trace (M3).} The metadata is a per-unit exponential moving
average (EMA)
\begin{equation}
m(t) = \left(1-\frac{1}{\tau_m}\right) m(t-1)
     + \frac{1}{\tau_m}\, x(t),
\label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
{\sloppy
with algorithmic timescale $\tau_m$ (pulses); the readout feature is
$\varphi(t) = [x(t),\, m(t),\, 1]$.\par}

\textbf{Readout.} OnlineRLS with forgetting $\lambda_f=0.999$ (weight
integration time $\tau_f = 1/(1-\lambda_f)=1000$ pulses),
predict-before-update, Tikhonov-regularized.

\textbf{Tasks and metrics.} \emph{regime\_switch}: three regimes with
overlapping pulse-interval distributions, regime changes every
$L=1500$ pulses; single pulses are almost indistinguishable across regimes
--- only long-run statistics discriminate. \emph{Mackey--Glass online
prediction} under a sequential disturbance chain. Metrics: overall
accuracy; switch-relative adaptation time $T_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ (pulses
after a \emph{known} switch to reach a 200- or 40-pulse window accuracy of
0.98/0.95); the normalized mean-squared error (NMSE); and the Jaeger
held-out memory capacity (MC) probe \cite{jaeger2002mc,white2004}.

\textbf{Sequential disturbance chain (substrate-agnostic).} Three
cumulative disturbances at $t=7$k/14k/21k pulses: (1)~timescale drift
(substrate $\tau \times 1.5$; ESN leaking rate $/1.5$), (2)~structure
prune (40\% of connections), (3)~readout noise ($\sigma=0.1$ on the
features). Per-round MC is probed at the settled physics. Initialization,
warm-up, and run-exclusion conventions are those of the released scripts
(Code and data availability); per-seed paired statistics are reported
throughout so that robustness can be checked directly.

\section{Theory: the slow trace is a synthesized forgetting kernel}
\label{sec:theory}

\textbf{Proposition 1 (kernel synthesis).} The input-to-slow-trace channel
implements a forgetting kernel with a zero at zero lag, a finite peak lag,
and an exponential tail whose $1/e$ horizon is exactly $\tau_m$ --- an
externally controllable, substrate-independent parameter. If the fast
state has kernel $h_x$, the slow channel kernel is the convolution of the
EMA impulse response with $h_x$; for $h_x(u)=e^{-u/\tau_x}$,
\begin{equation}
h_m(\Delta t)
= \frac{e^{-\Delta t/\tau_m} - e^{-\Delta t/\tau_x}}{\tau_m - \tau_x},
\qquad h_m(0)=0,\quad \mathrm{peak}\sim
\frac{\tau_m\tau_x}{\tau_m-\tau_x}\ln\frac{\tau_m}{\tau_x}.
\label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
The material kernel of Paper~A, $M(\Delta t)=\int p(\tau)\,e^{-\Delta
t/\tau}\,d\tau$ (log-normal), is fixed by physics; $h_m$ is synthesized
by the algorithm, and the augmented system's effective kernel is
$M \circledast h_m$ with $1/e$ horizon $\sim \tau_m + \tau_0$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:kernel}).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{paperC_fig1_kernel}
\caption{Synthesized vs.\ material forgetting kernel: the slow-trace
kernel $h_m$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:kernel}, $\tau_m = 200/500/1000$ pulses) has a
zero at zero lag, a finite peak lag, and an exponential tail with $1/e$
horizon exactly $\tau_m$; the material kernel $M$ (log-normal, CV 0.20)
is fixed by physics (1/e $\sim$16 pulses).}
\label{fig:kernel}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Proposition 2 (timescale coverage).} On the statistical task the
bottleneck is missing slow modes, not substrate physics. Both fast channels
decorrelate at $\tau_{\mathrm{eff}} \ll L$; the slow trace supplies the
missing timescale to each substrate, so accuracy and adaptation converge
to a common band. Measured (10 seeds): esn-fast 0.9955, esn-dual
0.9979, REDEM 0.9942; steady accuracy 1.000 for all (Table~\ref{tab:s10}).
The substrate-side fast-readout (metadata-free) comparison is not
re-tabulated in this table; it is reported in the companion papers, so
the substrate-side half of this claim rests on that evidence.

{\sloppy
\textbf{Proposition 3 (feature stationarity).} Post-switch adaptation is
bounded by the slower of feature re-centering ($\sim \tau_m + \tau_{\rm
eff}$) and weight re-integration ($\sim \tau_f$). The slow trace makes
recovery feature-limited rather than weight-limited. Under a controlled
switch-relative protocol, the true effect is modest but real: on the
substrate the fast readout needs $T_{200}=265$--$304$ pulses to
re-stabilize vs.\ $202$--$211$ for dual (factor 1.25--1.50 on $T_{200}$,
1.54--2.73 on $T_{40}$, as ratios of the Table~\ref{tab:adapt} endpoints);
on the ESN, $T_{40}$ drops from 49.9 to 40.6 with
the p90 collapsing from 76.5 to 42.0 (Table~\ref{tab:adapt}). The
``$9$--$20\times$'' ratios published in companion Paper~B were ratios of
window positions with near-zero denominators (metric artifacts), not
pulse times.\par}

\textbf{Proposition 4 (noise geometry).} The EMA is a first-order low-pass
filter, $|H(\omega)|^2 = 1/(1+(\omega\tau_m)^2)$. Short fast-channel
transients are attenuated in the metadata channel. The transferable value
of this is \emph{denoising of state-level corruption}: when the noise
enters the states before the slow trace is computed, the metadata strongly
attenuates it in our experiments (variant V2 of Section~\ref{sec:s16b});
we do not characterize the bandwidth of the injected state noise, so the
low-pass magnitude predicts the \emph{direction} of this effect but not
its exact size. It does \emph{not} protect the memory-capacity probe
against readout-level noise that hits the metadata channel equally
(variant V0); the prediction-error (NMSE) attenuation of readout noise,
by contrast, does transfer (Section~\ref{sec:s16}) --- a metric-level
distinction we make explicit to avoid conflating the two readout-noise
notions.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\textbf{Three mechanisms, three roles.} The evidence assembles a clean
decomposition. (i)~\emph{Metadata (M3) = statistical memory}: it supplies
the missing timescale on statistical tasks (Section~\ref{sec:s10}),
denoises state-level corruption (Section~\ref{sec:s16b}, V2), and
attenuates readout noise on the online task (Sections~\ref{sec:s14}--\ref{sec:s16})
--- but it adds no episodic memory capacity (r0 MC unchanged) and cannot
recover memory under readout-level disturbance at any tested $\tau_m$
(Sections~\ref{sec:s14}--\ref{sec:s16b}). (ii)~\emph{Homeostat (M5) =
disturbance recovery}: the $+32\%$ sequential recovery and the $+18\%$
$r_1\!\to\!r_3$ gain are produced by $\kappa$-regulation alone; the
metadata is not involved. (iii)~\emph{Substrate physics = raw memory}: the
MC probe puts the substrate at 10.19 vs.\ $\sim$6.17 for either ESN arm;
no algorithm in this work closes that lead. This decomposition is
established for the transfer directions tested here
(metadata$\to$ESN, routing$\to$Transformer); the reverse substitutions
--- the homeostat or the substrate physics taking over the metadata's
job, or vice versa --- were not tested.

\textbf{Why non-transferability is informative.} Each mechanism has a
precisely located job, and swapping them fails in a measurable way. This
delineates the boundary conditions of ``metadata transfer'' claims in the
online-reservoir literature: transfer holds for accuracy and
state-noise denoising, and does not hold for disturbance recovery or
episodic memory. A related boundary reproduces on a Transformer substrate
with low-rank adapters (Section~\ref{sec:llm}): routing transfers,
gating does not. The reproduction is qualitative, not pointwise: the
reservoir MC gap is flat in $\tau_m$, whereas the Transformer
stream-perplexity benefit reverses sign at $\tau_m\ge1000$.

\textbf{Related work.} The slow trace is a fixed first-order low-pass
operator on reservoir states; its goal --- extracting slowly varying
structure --- is in the spirit of slow feature analysis \cite{wiskott2002},
although, unlike SFA, it involves no learned whitening or optimization
step. The Jaeger held-out MC probe used throughout follows the
short-term-memory line of reservoir research
\cite{jaeger2002mc,white2004,hermans2010,verstraeten2007}, and the
fast/slow pair is a two-timescale memory hierarchy echoing complementary
learning systems \cite{mcclelland1995}, synaptic consolidation
\cite{benna2016}, and gated multi-timescale architectures
\cite{hochreiter1997,pascanu2013}; the readout computes a fading-memory
functional \cite{boyd1985} whose horizon the metadata extends without
touching the substrate, in the broader tradition of echo-state online
readouts \cite{lukosevicius2012,yildiz2012,sussillo2009} and physical
reservoir computing \cite{appeltant2011,larger2012,tanaka2019}. We use
``metadata'' for the slow trace's stored running statistics (a per-unit
EMA), not for meta-learning in the learning-to-learn sense
\cite{thrun1998,finn2017,hospedales2022}: nothing about the metadata's
update rule is itself learned here. In the continual-learning setting
\cite{parisi2019,kirkpatrick2017,hadsell2020,wang2024cl,dohare2024,ke2023cpt},
this work supplies causal evidence about \emph{which} component must carry
each capability.

\textbf{Limitations.} The slow trace's attenuation covers fast-channel
(readout or state) corruption; direct corruption of the metadata memory
itself is out of scope. $\tau_m$ remains a hyperparameter tuned to the
task timescale. The substrate's homeostat requires a Lyapunov estimate
(FTLE) that we do not provide for the ESN. Statistically, the core
falsification results rest on paired sign tests at $n=10$ seeds: they
are robust as far as they go (0/10 seeds positive in every tested cell)
but coarse --- at this sample size only large standardized effects (of
order $d\approx0.9$) are reliably detectable, and no formal effect
sizes, confidence intervals, or power analysis are reported; the
non-transferability claims should therefore be read as ``at every
tested configuration'' rather than as population-level universals.

\textbf{Future work.} Consolidating stable, repeatedly encountered
knowledge into the structural connectivity would create a hierarchy
between fast tracking and slow accumulation, but introduces a
consolidation-vs-overwrite trade-off beyond the present scope. Second,
Section~\ref{sec:llm} instantiates the same online readout on a
Transformer substrate through low-rank adaptation (LoRA-style) adapters; there the
recursive-least-squares update cost is $O(F^2)$ in the readout
\emph{feature} dimension $F$ --- the model width (thousands) or an
adapter rank (tens) --- not $O(P^2)$ in the total parameter count (tens
of billions), so the full-rank bottleneck does not arise at any scale
tested here.

\section{Extension to Transformer substrates: routing transfers, gating does not}
\label{sec:llm}

\subsection{Motivation and scope}

The three mechanisms are formulated substrate-agnostically, but all
evidence so far comes from reservoirs. We ask whether the decomposition
survives on a structurally different substrate --- a Transformer with
low-rank adaptation (LoRA) adapters \cite{hu2022lora} --- under the same falsification
discipline (10 seeds, paired per-seed differences, sign consistency). The
scope claim is deliberately narrow: the \emph{principles} instantiate on
a Transformer substrate; we make no benchmark or state-of-the-art (SOTA)
claims. Accordingly, every host-boundary statement in this paper is
confined to the two stateful substrates tested here; we draw no
conclusions about frozen or feed-forward hosts. The model
is tiny by design --- sized to run 90 seeded trials on CPU, not to
compete with large models.

\subsection{Model, task, and arms}

A character-level transformer \cite{vaswani2017} with $d=64$, two layers,
two heads, context 128, and a vocabulary of 32 ($\sim10^5$ parameters),
trained online on a streaming task with known switch instants. Two
biased-bigram Markov domains alternate every 3000 tokens (6 segments,
18\,k tokens): domain A biases the shift-1 bigram toward symbols 0--7;
domain B biases the shift-7 bigram toward symbols 24--31. Single tokens
are nearly indistinguishable across domains --- only bigram statistics
discriminate (the analogue of the timescale gap of
Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Embeddings, positions, and layer norms are
frozen; the output head is a trainable readout; two low-rank (rank-16)
adapters on the query/key/value (QKV), output, and feed-forward
(FFN) projections carry the trainable plasticity.

\textbf{Arms.} \emph{A1 (bare online LoRA):} both adapters update every
batch at a fixed learning rate --- the online-readout analogue with M1
always on. \emph{A2 (drift gate):} a slow exponential trace of the hidden
states (M3, Eq.~\ref{eq:ema}) estimates the current domain; the adapter
update is boosted for a window after a detected switch and suppressed
within a domain --- the ``when to adapt'' claim. \emph{A3 (domain
routing):} the same slow-trace estimate selects the active adapter, and
only the active one updates --- the ``which adapter to adapt'' claim.
$\tau_m\in\{200,500,1000,2000\}$.

\textbf{Metrics.} Stream perplexity (ppl, overall); forgetting perplexity
(perplexity on the previous domain after switching away --- the
episodic-memory probe); post-switch adaptation time in tokens (known
switches, 20-token window, $1.5\times$ steady-state threshold).

\subsection{Results: routing transfers, gating does not}

Table~\ref{tab:s18} and Fig.~\ref{fig:llm}. A3 (routing) transfers:
forgetting improves at \emph{every} tested $\tau_m$ (9--10/10 seeds, up to
$-2.51$ ppl, $-28\%$ at $\tau_m{=}200$), and stream perplexity improves
when the trace is fast ($\tau_m\in\{200,500\}$: $-1.07$/$-0.43$, 10/10
seeds). A2 (gating-only) is falsified: stream perplexity is worse than
A1 at every tested $\tau_m$ (paired differences $+1.25$ to $+2.29$, 0/10 seeds
positive) and forgetting never improves (0/10). The stream-ppl benefit
of A3 is timescale-sensitive: at $\tau_m\ge1000$ its sign flips. This is
\emph{not} the reservoir result restated: the reservoir MC gap was
\emph{insensitive} to $\tau_m$ at every tested timescale, with no
sensitive interval (Section~\ref{sec:s16}). The two findings differ in
metric (stream perplexity vs.\ memory capacity), in the compared
quantity (paired difference vs.\ A1 vs.\ gap to a fixed baseline) and in
mechanism, and must not be read as one shared interval. $T_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ floors at the 20-token
window for every arm: the task transition is learnable within one chunk,
so adaptation is too fast to resolve --- reported at the floor, not as
ratios (the Section~\ref{sec:adapt} lesson). The gate detector is
designed to track the five known switches of the protocol, and we read
the A2 failure as a failure of the update \emph{policy} rather than of
the detector; however, detector output was never compared against
switch ground truth (no detection-latency or miss statistics are
reported), so this attribution is plausible but not directly evidenced.

\subsection{Discussion: detection without continuous correction is insufficient}

The falsification of A2 is the informative result. Gating-only
instantiates the metadata as a detector (M3) while suppressing the
readout between detected switches: it removes M1's continuous online
correction and replaces gradual switching with an abrupt boost/suppress
policy. On the streaming task this loses twice --- within-domain
knowledge decays between switches (forgetting never improves, 0/10) and
the stream pays for the suppressed updates ($+1.25$ to $+2.29$ ppl).
This is the direct analogue of the reservoir finding that the metadata
supplies statistical memory but not episodic memory
(Section~\ref{sec:s14}): a detector does not learn. The general design
principle is that \emph{detection without continuous online correction
is insufficient}; the readout must remain active throughout the stream
to maintain within-domain knowledge, and switching must be gradual
(M4's ``gentle wins''), not abrupt. The A3 contrast locates the
transferable instantiation precisely: the slow trace's value lies in
\emph{selecting which plasticity is active}, not in \emph{deciding when
to pause learning}. The readout cost is feature-bound ($O(F^2)$ in model
width or adapter rank, not $O(P^2)$ in total parameters;
Section~\ref{sec:disc}). A protocol caveat: the arms differ in how many
adapter updates they take by construction (A2 suppresses updates within
a domain, A3 updates only the active adapter), and gradient-step budgets
were not explicitly matched to A1, so A2's failure could partly reflect
reduced update counts; the sign consistency of the paired differences
across all tested $\tau_m$ and both metrics makes a pure training-budget
artifact unlikely, but a budget-matched control was not run.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

(1)~A slow exponential trace of reservoir states is a synthesized
forgetting kernel with a controllable horizon (Prop.~1). (2)~On
long-horizon statistical tasks the bottleneck is timescale coverage, not
substrate physics; the same trace brings ESN and substrate accuracy into
a common band and
accelerates adaptation by a controlled-measured factor 1.25--2.7
(Props.~2--3). (3)~The trace is a \emph{statistical} memory, not an
episodic one: it adds no raw memory capacity and does not transfer
disturbance robustness at any tested $\tau_m$ (0/10 seeds positive; Prop.~4).

(4)~Three mechanisms, three roles --- metadata (statistical memory and
state-noise denoising), homeostat (sequential recovery), substrate physics
(raw memory) --- and none is substitutable for another's job in the
directions tested.

(5)~The decomposition survives on a Transformer substrate with
low-rank adapters: slow-trace domain routing transfers (forgetting up to
$-28\%$, stream perplexity $-1.07$ at $\tau_m{=}200$; 10/10 seeds; the
stream-perplexity benefit holds only at $\tau_m\le500$ and reverses sign
at $\tau_m\ge1000$) while
a gating-only variant is falsified at every tested $\tau_m$ (0/10 seeds) ---
detection without continuous correction is insufficient.
(6)~That boundary is a property of the \emph{host}, not of the
mechanisms: routing transfers on a Transformer substrate (point~5), and
the failure of abrupt gating is plausibly attributable to its update
policy (Section~6.3 offers this as an interpretation, not a directly
evidenced result) ---
removing the continuously active readout (M1) and replacing gradual
structural switching (M4) with an abrupt one. The reach of this claim is
bounded by what we tested: both hosts examined are stateful (a recurrent
reservoir; a Transformer with trainable adapters and a persistent
hidden-state trace), and the one successful transfer (A3) succeeds
precisely on the host that keeps its readout continuously active. We
therefore do \emph{not} claim a general host boundary: the reading that
the full framework ``does not instantiate on a frozen-feedforward host''
extrapolated beyond the evidence, since no frozen or feed-forward host
was tested here. The supported conclusion is narrower --- on the tested
stateful hosts, and under the abrupt-gating update policy, the
mechanism set does not transfer as-is --- and whether the requirements
(a continuously active online readout, a slowly integrating statistical
memory, gradually applied structural switches) can be met by frozen or
feed-forward architectures is an open question that we leave to future
work.
